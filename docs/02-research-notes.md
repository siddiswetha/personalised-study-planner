# 02 — Research Notes

## Personalised Study Planner

---

## Purpose

To understand how real students plan revision today, where the plan breaks, and what "a good plan" means to them — before designing the planner.

This document is a research plan and a recording template. It is not a set of findings yet.

**All interview questions below are templates. All example answers are labelled EXAMPLE. Do not present template or example material as real user quotes.**

---

## Research plan

### Participants

Interview **5 students across different courses / years**.

Aim for variety, so the planner is not designed for one type of student only. For example:

- Different courses or subjects
- Different years of study
- At least one student with a part-time job or heavy fixed commitments
- At least one student with exams close together

### How to recruit

- Ask classmates, friends, or study groups.
- Explain the purpose in one sentence: "I am designing a study planner and I want to learn how you plan revision today."
- Ask for permission before the interview, and before collecting any timetable or syllabus.
- Keep every participant anonymous in this document. Use labels such as Student A, Student B — never real names.

### Data set to collect (with permission)

For each student who agrees, collect:

- Their real exam timetable
- Their syllabus, with topics and weightage where available
- How many hours per day they really have for study

Store these separately and securely. Do not paste personal details into this document. Note only that the material was collected, and what it covers in general terms.

---

## Interview guide — template

Use the same core questions for each student, so answers can be compared. Follow up with "Can you tell me more about that?" when an answer is interesting.

### Part 1 — How they plan today

1. Walk me through how you plan your revision for exams. Where do you start?
2. Do you use a timetable, a list, an app, or nothing at all?
3. How do you decide what to study on a given day?
4. How do you decide how much time to give each subject?

### Part 2 — Where the plan breaks

5. Tell me about a time your plan did not work. What happened?
6. What happens when you miss a day — illness, work, family, or just exhaustion?
7. What happens when two exams are close together?
8. Which topics do you leave until last? Why those?

### Part 3 — What "a good plan" means

9. What would a good plan look like to you? What would it include?
10. How much detail do you want in a daily plan? What is too much?
11. How do you know if you are prepared for an exam?
12. What would make you trust a plan enough to follow it for a week?

### Part 4 — Time and commitments

13. How many hours per day can you really study on a normal day? On a busy day?
14. What fixed commitments do you have — classes, work, family, travel?
15. What does a break look like for you? How often do you need one?

## Research ethics — simple rules

- Ask permission before every interview and before collecting any timetable or syllabus.
- Keep participants anonymous. Use Student A–E, never real names, in any shared document.
- Do not share a student's timetable or syllabus with anyone else without their permission.
- If a student talks about stress, health, or personal problems, listen kindly — but do not offer medical or mental-health advice. The planner is a planning aid, not a counsellor. See `docs/07-risks-guardrails.md`.
- Let the student stop the interview at any time.
